Fourteen years of practice. Zero dollars of startup money.

I made my first jar of jam when I was 16. My mom is from Costa Rica, my dad is from here, and my kitchen is where those two worlds have always met. I've lived in Dallas my whole life and I love this city.

I come from humble beginnings. Everything I have, I worked for. I'll be honest with you: life has been hard, and some seasons have been genuinely bitter. But I'm a fighter, and making bitter jam turned out to be how I take something hard and turn it into something people want to eat.

I have the recipe, the skills, the certification, and the plan. What I don't have is the money to start.

That's the whole reason this page exists. Not for a handout — for a start. A stand, a stack of jars, the fruit to fill them, and the permits to sell them legally. After that, the jam does the work.

Why it's called Amargo

Amargo is Spanish for bitter. Most jam is sweet and then it's over. Mine is sweet up front with a real bitter finish, closer to a proper English marmalade than to grape jelly. The name is a joke about my life and a promise about the flavor at the same time.

This isn't your grandma's grape jelly.

Small-batch lemon and blackberry, cooked in my own kitchen and preserved the old-fashioned way so it keeps. Sweet on the front of your tongue, bitter and bright on the finish, the way the good marmalades in a London shop taste.
